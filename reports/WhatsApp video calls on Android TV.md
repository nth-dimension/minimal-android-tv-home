# Shrink the pixels before buying a box

Software can make WhatsApp video calls on this BRAVIA smoother, but only up to a hard ceiling. The cheapest way to get there is to make the WebView wrapper feed WhatsApp's desktop-tuned calling engine fewer pixels, both going in and coming out. WhatsApp Web calling runs its own WebAssembly VoIP engine: a reported 20-worker thread pool, H.264 video, and RTP tunnelled through a WebRTC DataChannel. Because of that, the browser's RTP controls (bitrate caps, codec preferences, degradation preference) have nothing to act on. A wrapper can still control capture size and frame rate, how many pixels WebView renders, codec latency hints, and memory pressure. The first two fixes are free. First, clamp the camera to 640x360 or 640x480 at 15 fps, where the Brio gives about 16 fps on the Sony's camera stack instead of about 11.5 fps at 720p. Second, render the page at 960x540, either with two WebView settings or with a no-root `cmd game downscale` override. Both rendering recipes come from reading the Chromium and Android source code and have not been tested on the TV yet. The native WhatsApp Android app, linked as a companion device, is a legitimate one-hour experiment that costs nothing. But nobody knows whether its call screen will accept a USB camera that Android labels "external", and the closest recent report (ChromeOS, 2025) failed. Every route that runs on the TV also needs a new microphone, because the Brio's USB audio function never reached Android. A ₹5,799 speakerphone with built-in echo cancellation is the robust fix, as long as the TV sends call audio out through it. If tuning still leaves the TV with more than three of its four cores busy, stop tuning. No Android TV box sold in India is clearly faster than the TV. The better answers are mirroring a phone (₹0) or an Intel N100 mini PC from about ₹11,500, which is the only big-screen route WhatsApp officially supports.

## WhatsApp's own call engine leaves the wrapper four real levers

WhatsApp Web calling is new and officially narrow. It entered beta in February 2026 and was announced for everyone on **28 July 2026**. The launch added group calls, improved noise suppression and "QuickHD", which makes calls "shift to higher-resolution video faster when network conditions support it" ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-whatsapp-web-browser-voice-video-calling); [WhatsApp Blog](https://blog.whatsapp.com/introducing-web-calling-on-whatsapp-plus-more-new-updates)). WhatsApp's Help Center lists web calling only on **Windows, Linux and macOS**, and Android is not listed ([WhatsApp FAQ: web calling](https://faq.whatsapp.com/2092488194268012); [supported browsers](https://faq.whatsapp.com/803865374003522)). So the wrapper, an Android WebView pretending to be desktop Chrome, sits outside the supported setup, and WhatsApp's defaults assume a desktop's spare capacity.

What runs inside the page is not ordinary browser WebRTC. Community reverse engineers report that the calling stack is WhatsApp's native C++ VoIP engine compiled to WebAssembly with Emscripten. Its logs show `pjlib 2.13` and an Opus codec starting up, and JavaScript registers audio capture and playback drivers for it ([hisalman.in](https://hisalman.in/blog/reverse-engineering-whatsapp-calls); [WACRG wasm analysis](https://wacrg.org/techniques/wasm-analysis)). The browser's RTCPeerConnection serves only as a transport. Media is RTP carried inside a DataChannel to WhatsApp's relay servers, encrypted with SRTP and SFrame inside the WASM ([WACRG video packetization](https://wacrg.org/spec/encodings/video-packetization)). Two independent reimplementations, one in Node and one in Go, reach WhatsApp's relays exactly this way ([baileys-caller-optimized](https://github.com/LuxyTeam/baileys-caller-optimized/blob/main/README.md); [WaCalls](https://github.com/JotaDev66/WaCalls)). According to the WACRG draft spec, video **must be H.264**, and it goes through a passthrough packetizer rather than an encoder built into the WASM ([WACRG video](https://wacrg.org/spec/encodings/video)). The Node reimplementation also reports that WhatsApp's browser thread pool is **20 workers**, and that cutting it to 4 in Node shrank memory use from about 430 MB to about 160 MB ([baileys-caller-optimized](https://github.com/LuxyTeam/baileys-caller-optimized/blob/main/README.md)). That pool would explain why WhatsApp requires SharedArrayBuffer and the cluster of worker threads (~0.3 core) seen on the TV. It probably also contributes to the 850 MB renderer that ran the 3 GB TV out of memory (project measurements, 2026-10-20). Treat all of this as probable, not proven. Meta has published nothing about web calling, WACRG labels most of its findings "draft", and it lists congestion control and quality adaptation as not yet understood ([WACRG media-srtp](https://wacrg.org/media-srtp)).

As a result, a whole category of planned fixes does nothing. The browser sends no RTP of its own, so `RTCRtpSender.setParameters` (maxBitrate, scaleResolutionDownBy, maxFramerate, degradationPreference), `setCodecPreferences` and track `contentHint` have nothing to act on. The planned switch to H.264 for hardware encoding is moot too, because WhatsApp's protocol already uses H.264. Diagnostics change as well. The browser's CPU-overuse detector and the `qualityLimitationReason` stat exist only for browser RTP streams ([W3C webrtc-stats](https://www.w3.org/TR/webrtc-stats/)), so `window.__tvchatStats()` will probably report no outgoing video at all. The useful stats become `data-channel` bytes per second, `candidate-pair` round-trip time and `transport` counters. One detail matters for jitter. DataChannels run over SCTP, which has TCP-style congestion control ([RFC 8831](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8831.pdf)). If WhatsApp opens its channel as ordered and reliable (undocumented), each lost Wi-Fi packet would hold up everything behind it, and video would arrive in bursts even with plenty of bandwidth. Logging the `createDataChannel` options would settle this.

Four levers remain. **Capture constraints are the strongest.** On the Sony's MediaTek camera stack, the Brio delivers about **16 fps at 640x360 or 640x480, but only about 11.5 fps at 848x480 and above**, and less in dim light (project measurements). The wrapper currently caps capture at 1280x720. Clamping getUserMedia to a native 640x360 or 640x480 mode at 15 fps gives the engine about 40% more frames, each with a third to a quarter of the pixels. The wrapper should also intercept `applyConstraints`, because QuickHD may ask for a bigger size mid-call. Far-end jitter starts on the sending side, so an uneven capture rate and CPU starvation (3.4 of 4 cores busy) are more likely culprits than the codec.

The second lever applies only if WhatsApp encodes and decodes video through WebCodecs. That is an inference from an Android hardware-codec thread ("CodecLooper") visible during calls, not a confirmed fact. If it holds, a script injected at page start can force `hardwareAcceleration: "prefer-hardware"` and `latencyMode: "realtime"` on `VideoEncoder.configure` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/VideoEncoder/configure)), and `optimizeForLatency: true` on the decoder. It must never change width, height or codec, which the packetizer and the far end expect. Third, the audio-processing constraints govern the ~0.2-core audio input thread, which ties into the microphone choice below. Fourth, memory. One speculative idea is to shrink the 20-worker pool by faking `navigator.hardwareConcurrency`. It works only if WhatsApp sizes the pool from that value, and too small a pool could stall the engine.

MediaTek's codec record cuts both ways. Chromium's Android encoder always prefers a hardware encoder, and only H.264 may fall back to Android's software encoder, `c2.android.avc.encoder` ([ndk_video_encode_accelerator.cc](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/media/gpu/android/ndk_video_encode_accelerator.cc)). A MediaTek bug that caused exactly that silent fallback is fixed ([crbug 409110228](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/409110228)). Decoding is worse. An open Chromium bug shows MediaTek devices picking the normal H.264 decoder instead of the low-latency one, with **15 ms+ per-frame decode times** still reported in May 2026, even with the `media-codec-low-delay-mode` flag on ([crbug 439294798](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/439294798)). That would add stutter to the video the TV receives, but it can't explain what the far end sees. Two quick checks help here. Searching `logcat` during a call for `c2.mtk.avc.encoder` versus `c2.android.avc.encoder` shows whether the hardware encoder is in use. Running `VideoEncoder.isConfigSupported()` with an H.264 640x360 hardware real-time configuration inside the WebView shows whether a hardware H.264 encoder is available to the page at all.

## Rendering at 960x540 attacks the half-core painting bill

Page painting is the largest call cost the wrapper controls: about **0.5 core**, spread across WebView's render thread, its compositor and the Mali GPU thread (project measurements). The source code now explains why the earlier attempt to halve it failed. On Android 12, Chromium builds its own window context for the display from the *Application* context and reads screen density from that ([PhysicalDisplayAndroid.java](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/ui/android/java/src/org/chromium/ui/display/PhysicalDisplayAndroid.java)). Android 12 window contexts inherit no density overrides an app sets on its own Context ([ContextImpl.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/core/java/android/app/ContextImpl.java)). So WebView never saw the density override (the ContextThemeWrapper): devicePixelRatio stayed 2, and a 960x540-pixel view became a 480x270 page. No WebView or androidx.webkit setting changes devicePixelRatio. Chromium's `--force-device-scale-factor` switch does work in code, but WebView reads command-line flags only on eng/userdebug builds ([CommandLineUtil.java](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/android_webview/java/src/org/chromium/android_webview/common/CommandLineUtil.java); [WebView command-line docs](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/android_webview/docs/commandline-flags.md)), and the TV is a production `user` build (device probe, 2026-10-20).

Two cheaper routes remain, both untested on the TV. **Recipe A** keeps the existing half-size WebView, scaled up 2x, and adds two settings: `useWideViewPort=false` and `setInitialScale(100)`. `setInitialScale` "doesn't take the screen density into account" ([WebView.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/core/java/android/webkit/WebView.java)), so at devicePixelRatio 2 WebView turns 100% into a page zoom of 0.5 ([aw_settings.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/android_webview/browser/aw_settings.cc)). Blink's WebView rules then lay the page out 480 ÷ 0.5 = **960 CSS px** wide, with the height following the aspect ratio ([page_scale_constraints_set.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/frame/page_scale_constraints_set.cc)). The page is then drawn at 2 × 0.5 = 1 device pixel per CSS pixel. WebView ignores the View's 2x scale when drawing and uses it only to decide which tiles to draw first ([synchronous_layer_tree_frame_sink.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/widget/compositing/android_webview/synchronous_layer_tree_frame_sink.cc)). So tiles are drawn at 960x540, roughly **a quarter of the pixels**, and tile memory drops by about the same factor.

The side effects are manageable:
- `devicePixelRatio` still reports 2, so responsive images (`srcset`) and any canvas sized by devicePixelRatio still get double-resolution backing stores.
- Pinch zoom must be turned off.
- D-pad cursor events injected straight into the WebView need their coordinates halved.

WhatsApp's viewport tag could not be read (curl got an error page), so check in DevTools that `innerWidth` is about 960 and `visualViewport.scale` is 0.5.

The **Game Mode downscale override** needs no code at all: `adb shell cmd game downscale 0.5 dev.minimal.tvchat`, reverted with `cmd game downscale disable dev.minimal.tvchat` ([GameManagerShellCommand.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/core/java/com/android/server/app/GameManagerShellCommand.java)). In Android 12 the downscale applies whether or not the app is a game ([CompatModePackages.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/core/java/com/android/server/wm/CompatModePackages.java)). On production builds, the shell may apply it if it holds the permission `OVERRIDE_COMPAT_CHANGE_CONFIG_ON_RELEASE_BUILD` ([OverrideValidatorImpl.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/core/java/com/android/server/compat/OverrideValidatorImpl.java)), and the Sony's shell does (device probe). According to the source, the app window is then laid out at 960x540, WebView sees a real devicePixelRatio of 1, and the system scales the small image up to the panel.

There are trade-offs. Applying the override kills the app ([PlatformCompat.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/core/java/com/android/server/compat/PlatformCompat.java)), and it affects the whole app, including the cursor and popups. Google's guidance is the main caution: window downscaling works on "select Android 12 devices", child windows "may not be resized correctly", and Google recommends **70% or higher**. Google quotes savings of up to 30% GPU and 10% system power, measured on games ([Game Mode interventions](https://developer.android.com/games/optimize/adpf/gamemode/gamemode-interventions)). So try 0.5, fall back to 0.7 if popups misbehave, and check whether the setting survives a reboot (unverified). Prefer this route over Recipe A if logging shows WhatsApp drawing video into a canvas sized by devicePixelRatio, because only the downscale makes devicePixelRatio truly 1.

The remaining options are worse trades:
- **WebView on a virtual display.** A private 960x540, 160-dpi virtual display holding the WebView (VirtualDisplay plus Presentation) is the off-screen method Chromium itself documents ([software_draw_deprecated.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/android_webview/docs/software_draw_deprecated.md)), and it gives an exact devicePixelRatio of 1. Flutter's experience with the same design shows the cost: input has to be re-created by hand, the display always counts as unfocused, which breaks text input, and accessibility is fragile ([Flutter Virtual-Display.md](https://flutter.googlesource.com/mirrors/flutter/+/HEAD/docs/platforms/android/Virtual-Display.md)).
- **Software rendering.** It breaks `<video>` outright ([software_draw_deprecated.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/android_webview/docs/software_draw_deprecated.md)).
- **System-wide resolution change.** `wm size 960x540` plus `wm density 160` would cut every app's pixels by four but blur the whole TV. Also, `wm size reset` may restore the panel's native 3840x2160 instead of Sony's 1920x1080 setting, so revert to 1920x1080 explicitly.
- **WebView DevTools flags.** This is the only flag screen on production builds. It opens with `adb shell am start -a "com.android.webview.SHOW_DEV_UI"`, applies to every WebView app and survives reboots ([developer-ui.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/android_webview/docs/developer-ui.md)). It has no devicePixelRatio flag.

The DevTools list does include `WebViewSurfaceControlForTV`, which is **on by default** for TVs, and the VH21 is not on the list of excluded devices. So remote video may already go through a hardware video overlay, which spares the GPU ([gpu_finch_features.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/gpu/config/gpu_finch_features.cc); [ProductionSupportedFlagList.java](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/android_webview/java/src/org/chromium/android_webview/common/ProductionSupportedFlagList.java)). That only helps if WhatsApp renders remote video in a `<video>` element, which is unconfirmed. `adb shell dumpsys SurfaceFlinger --list` during a call shows whether extra overlay layers appear. One conflict between the research notes needs flagging: one suggests turning on `media-codec-low-delay-mode` from this screen, but the flag does not appear in the list read from Chromium's source, so WebView may not offer it.

Nobody has published WebView video-call measurements on MediaTek or Mali TVs. The only reports found are old, and they blame software video decoding as the usual CPU hog ([Arm community](https://community.arm.com/support-forums/f/graphics-gaming-and-vr-forum/8709/mali400-rockchip-rk3188)). A quarter of the pixels does not mean a quarter of the cost, because fixed per-frame work stays: preparing each frame, compositing, importing video frames and GPU driver overhead. Expect a real cut to the 0.5-core painting cost and some memory relief, but not a 4x win. Measure with `top -H` or Perfetto before and after each change.

## Native WhatsApp on the TV is a free test that hinges on one camera API

Linking the official Android app to an existing account is a supported feature, just not on TVs. Since 2023 the standard app can join an account as a "companion phone" or tablet: it shows a QR code and the main phone scans it. One main phone can link **up to four devices**, and voice and video calls are not among the features linked devices lose ([WhatsApp FAQ: linked devices](https://faq.whatsapp.com/378279804439436/?cms_platform=android); [companion phone steps](https://faq.whatsapp.com/1046791737425017); [WABetaInfo](https://wabetainfo.com/whatsapp-is-releasing-the-companion-mode-for-all-beta-testers-on-android)). TVs are not on WhatsApp's list of supported linked devices, and the research found no dated first-hand report from 2024–2026 of linking on Android TV. Linking should need no SIM card, phone service or camera, since the TV only displays the code, but that is inferred. The TV app takes one of the four slots alongside the tvchat web session. Both log out if the main phone goes unused for 14 days ([WhatsApp FAQ: about linked devices](https://faq.whatsapp.com/general/download-and-installation/about-linked-devices)).

The camera is the crux, and WhatsApp documents nothing about it. The strongest positive clue comes from Android's own source. Android 12 reports any USB ("external") camera to apps using the older camera interface, Camera1, as a **front-facing** camera ([CameraProviderManager.cpp](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/av/+/refs/tags/android-12.0.0_r1/services/camera/libcameraservice/common/CameraProviderManager.cpp)). A 2021 Waydroid log shows WhatsApp opening camera 0 through Camera1 ([waydroid #170](https://github.com/waydroid/waydroid/issues/170)). The Brio is the Sony's only camera, and Camera1 apps already work on this TV (project notes), so if WhatsApp still uses Camera1 for calls, it would see one front camera.

The negative evidence is more recent:
- **ChromeOS, late 2025.** On a Chromebox and a Lenovo Chromebook, WhatsApp's Android app used three Logitech USB webcams' microphones but could not switch to their video, while the built-in camera worked ([Chromebook Community](https://support.google.com/chromebook/thread/376667759/usb-camera-s-not-working-in-some-android-apps?hl=en)). ChromeOS runs Android apps on a different camera stack, so this is only a rough stand-in.
- **Samsung DeX, April 2026.** A Reddit feature request asks WhatsApp to support external cameras ([r/SamsungDex](https://www.reddit.com/r/SamsungDex/comments/1slgmb4/feature_request_usbexternal_camera_support_for)), but Samsung phones don't expose USB webcams to apps anyway, so it proves little.
- **Modern camera libraries.** Google's CameraX treats choosing an external camera as experimental ([CameraSelector.Builder](https://developer.android.com/reference/androidx/camera/core/CameraSelector.Builder)), so an app that only looks for front and back cameras would skip the Brio.

The odds are genuinely even, and a ten-minute test settles it. Start a WhatsApp video call while watching `adb logcat | grep -i "CameraService::connect"`, which shows the camera ID and camera API version, and also run `adb shell dumpsys media.camera`. One Sony-specific risk applies. The MediaTek camera driver can get stuck in a permanent "VIDIOC_S_PARM busy" state after the camera is reconfigured (project notes), and WhatsApp opening the camera at its own sizes could trigger that. A reboot clears it.

Control is the second hurdle. WhatsApp's phone interface isn't built for the arrow-keys-and-select remote that Google requires of TV apps ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/training/tv/get-started/controllers)), so expect to use a pointer. The options are the Mouse Toggle accessibility service, which turns the D-pad into a cursor ([androidtvnews.com](https://androidtvnews.com/mouse-toggle-android-tv)), the open-source remouse ([GitHub](https://github.com/zy0816/remouse)), the touchpad mode in the Google TV phone app ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.google.android.tv.remote.service&hl=en_US)), or a cheap air mouse. Nobody has tested WhatsApp's in-call controls or incoming-call screen this way. The critical unknown is whether an incoming call rings on Google TV while WhatsApp is in the background. A low-authority guide claims notifications are delayed and video calls are portrait-only ([AndroidGuias](https://en.androidguias.com/How-to-install-WhatsApp-on-Android-TV-and-get-the-most-out-of-it/)); test that rather than trust it.

If the camera works, the payoff is large. A native call skips the WebView renderer, its compositor, the page's JavaScript and the 20-worker WASM engine. Meta built its mobile calling stack for weak hardware. It switches between H.264 and AV1 mid-call based on CPU load and encode time, it found software encoders often beat hardware ones at low resolutions, and it turned AV1 off on low-memory devices after out-of-memory crashes ([Meta Engineering, 2024](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/03/20/video-engineering/mobile-rtc-video-av1-hd)). Meta's June 2026 post says AV1 now runs on most mobile devices, using an ultra-light setting with automatic fallback to H.264, at typical bitrates of 10–400 kbps ([Meta Engineering, 2026](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/06/22/video-engineering/adopting-av1-for-real-time-communication-rtc-meta); [PPC Land summary](https://ppc.land/metas-av1-codec-now-runs-on-most-mobile-devices-for-video-calls/)). One research thread could not load the post itself, so some of these details rest on the summary. No benchmarks exist for native WhatsApp on any TV device. The camera's 11.5–16 fps ceiling also stays, so native would buy smoothness more than sharpness.

On WhatsApp's terms of service, the ban risk WhatsApp documents targets modified apps such as GB WhatsApp, and scraping ([WhatsApp FAQ: unofficial apps](https://faq.whatsapp.com/general/security-and-privacy/about-unofficial-apps)). Its ban page also mentions using an "unsupported device" ([WhatsApp FAQ: bans](https://faq.whatsapp.com/1064395290901991)), but the research found no case of enforcement against the official app on a TV. Install the app from whatsapp.com, never a modded build, and accept that updates will be manual. Arguably, tvchat's fake desktop browser identity is a greyer area than the official app.

## A phone or a ₹11,500 mini PC beats every Android TV box

The cheapest route off the TV's chip costs nothing. The VH21 accepts **AirPlay 2** mirroring from an iPhone and Google Cast mirroring from Android ([Sony Help Guide](https://helpguide.sony.net/tv/gusltn2/v1/en-003/05_04.html)). It does not accept Sony's Miracast-based "Screen mirroring", which Google TV models lack ([Sony Canada](https://www.sony.ca/en/electronics/support/televisions-projectors-lcd-tvs-android-/kd-55x75k/articles/00044548)). Indian how-to guides from Gadgets Now and Vijay Sales describe mirroring a WhatsApp video call to the TV, with the phone handling camera and microphone ([Gadgets Now](https://gadgetsnow.indiatimes.com/faqs/how-do-i-connect-my-whatsapp-to-my-tv/articleshow/83597127.cms); [Vijay Sales](https://www.vijaysales.com/blog-listing/how-to-make-video-calls-on-smart-tv)). The phone's own app and hardware do all the work, and the TV just plays a video stream.

The trade-offs:
- WhatsApp's portrait interface shows with black bars on either side.
- The phone has to sit on a tripod near the TV.
- There is some lag.
- Battery drain: one tester measured **15–20% per hour** on an iPhone 14 ([The Tech Gorilla](https://thetechgorilla.com/how-to-mirror-iphone-to-tv)).

A cable avoids the Wi-Fi lag. iPhone 15, 16 and 17 models (not the "e" versions) mirror to HDMI over USB-C at up to 4K60 ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-am/105099)). Android phones need a USB-C video output (DisplayPort Alt Mode), which is common on flagships and rare on budget models ([Computer Cable Store](https://www.computercablestore.com/connect-android-phone-to-tv)). Samsung's wireless DeX needs a Miracast TV, which this Sony is not ([Samsung](https://www.samsung.com/us/support/answer/ANS10001955)). Caveat: WhatsApp documents none of this. No first-hand report from 2025–2026 confirmed that calls mirror without a black screen, and nobody has tested echo when the phone's mic hears the TV speakers.

A small Windows or Linux PC on HDMI is the only route on WhatsApp's supported list. Web calling covers Windows, Linux and macOS in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari ([WhatsApp FAQ](https://faq.whatsapp.com/2092488194268012)), and includes group calls of up to 32 people. Since July 2026 it also offers Call Transfer, which moves a call started on the phone to WhatsApp Web or Desktop; WhatsApp's wording mentions group calls, so whether it covers 1:1 calls is unclear ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/07/28/whatsapp-web-calling-new-features)). An **Intel N100** has four cores at up to 3.4 GHz and hardware H.264, HEVC and VP9 encoding ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2024/04/29/raspberry-pi-5-intel-n100-mini-pc-comparison-features-benchmarks-price)). It scores roughly 1,200 single-core and 3,100 multi-core in Geekbench 6, against about 900 and 2,150 for a Raspberry Pi 5 ([raspberry.tips](https://raspberry.tips/en/smart-home/home-assistant-hardware-2026-raspberry-pi-5-or-n100-mini-pc)). Amazon.in lists N100 mini PCs from **₹11,499** ([Amazon.in](https://www.amazon.in/n100-mini-pc/s?k=n100%20mini%20pc)); Smartprix listings above ₹57,000 for Beelink models look like inflated import prices ([Smartprix](https://www.smartprix.com/computers/beelink-brand)).

On a PC the Brio also escapes the Sony's camera stack. Linux machines see all of its video, microphone and control interfaces ([linux-hardware.org](https://linux-hardware.org/?probe=ff03f6aa77&log=lsusb)), and Logitech rates it at 1080p and 30 fps ([Brio 305 datasheet](https://www.logitech.com/content/dam/logitech/en_au/video-collaboration/pdf/brio-305-datasheet.pdf)). Use Chrome or Edge rather than the WhatsApp Windows app. The app is now just a browser wrapper (WebView2) around web.whatsapp.com, and it used about **900 MB of RAM and 25.8% CPU** during a video call in Windows Latest's test, which didn't state the CPU ([Windows Latest](https://www.windowslatest.com/2025/11/22/i-tested-the-new-whatsapp-for-windows-11-a-web-wrapper-and-its-a-performance-nightmare/)). Nobody has reported whether WhatsApp's engine uses Intel's hardware encoder, or how an N100 call actually performs, so this ranking rests on specs and official support. Echo with TV speakers and a webcam mic is still a question there too. An old laptop on HDMI is the ₹0 version of this route. A Raspberry Pi 5 counts as supported (it runs Linux), but it has no hardware H.264 encoder ([Raspberry Pi white paper](https://pip-assets.raspberrypi.com/categories/685-app-notes-guides-whitepapers/documents/RP-010033-WP-1-H.264%20encoding%20performance%20on%20Raspberry%20Pi%205_series%20computers.pdf)), and rising RAM prices have erased its price advantage ([raspberry.tips](https://raspberry.tips/en/smart-home/home-assistant-hardware-2026-raspberry-pi-5-or-n100-mini-pc)).

Android TV boxes fare poorly because they inherit both of the TV's problems: an unofficial WhatsApp route and a phone-class CPU. Any box would run either tvchat (the same CPU-hungry engine) or the sideloaded app (the same camera gamble). The research did not identify the Sony VH21's exact chip, so "same class" below is inferred from core type and count.

## The Brio's microphone never reached Android, so buy one that cancels its own echo

The Brio 300 has an ordinary microphone; the TV never received its description. When a USB device is plugged in, it sends the computer a descriptor listing its functions, which USB calls "interfaces". Four independent Linux dumps of the Brio's descriptor (USB ID 046d:0942) all show **five interfaces** ([linux-hardware.org probe](https://linux-hardware.org/?probe=ff03f6aa77&log=lsusb); [second probe](https://linux-hardware.org/?probe=1db191b4d3&log=lsusb)):
- video on interfaces 0–1;
- a standard USB microphone (USB Audio Class 1) on interfaces 2–3, mono, 16-bit, at 16, 32 or 48 kHz;
- a control (HID) interface on 4.

The descriptor is 2,230 bytes long and has no configuration name. The Sony reports only the two video interfaces, plus a configuration named "Brio 305" (`dumpsys usb` in the earlier session).

Android 12 is not hiding anything. Its only interface filter, `mBlockAudio`, is declared but never switched on, and would drop only audio-streaming interfaces anyway ([UsbConfigDescriptor.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/usb/java/com/android/server/usb/descriptors/UsbConfigDescriptor.java)). Android's USB library copies the kernel's raw bytes into a 4,096-byte buffer, which the descriptor easily fits ([usbhost.c](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/system/core/+/refs/heads/android12-release/libusbhost/usbhost.c)). The control interface vanished too, which means the TV received everything up to the end of the video section and nothing after it. That fits a cut-off or garbled read while the device is being set up. Logitech's C920 webcams had exactly this documented failure, "spewing random data" on the full-length read, and Linux added a delay workaround for them ([Linux commit e0429362ab15](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/e0429362ab15c46ea4d64c3f8c9e0933e48a143a)). Mainline Linux has no such workaround for the Brio 300 ([quirks.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/drivers/usb/core/quirks.c)). The other explanation is that the camera's firmware sent a different, video-only descriptor. Both are hypotheses. The user's earlier impression that the Sony saw "an external camera plus USB audio" hints that the result may change from one plug-in to the next.

The checks cost nothing and need no root:
- **Read the raw descriptor.** After replugging, run `adb shell dumpsys usb dump-descriptors -dump-raw` ([UsbService.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/usb/java/com/android/server/usb/UsbService.java)). On the firmware the PCs saw, bytes 20–21 should read `B6 08` (2,230 bytes), byte 22 should be 5 (interfaces) and byte 24 should be 0 (no name).
  - If the header is correct but the bytes stop or turn to garbage around byte 2,029, the read was garbled.
  - If the header itself says two interfaces, the camera sent a video-only descriptor.
- **Test the timing theory.** Replug 5–10 times across different ports, after a cold boot, after the TV has fully started, and through a powered hub.
- **Update the firmware.** Use Logi Tune on the Mac. Firmware 1.0.28 for the Brio 300/301/305 lists "Fixed the issue where mic was not working correctly", though nothing ties that note to this symptom ([Logitech release note](https://hub.sync.logitech.com/brio305/post/brio-300-301-305-1-0-28-dNpJyKUO4eUaSK5)).

If the mic ever appears, Android's USB audio support can handle its format ([alsa_device_profile.c](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/system/media/+/refs/heads/android12-release/alsa_utils/alsa_device_profile.c)). However, Android 12 uses only one USB audio device at a time, the last one plugged in, so a speakerphone added later would take over ([UsbAlsaManager.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/android12-release/services/usb/java/com/android/server/usb/UsbAlsaManager.java); [AOSP audio routing](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/combined-audio-routing)).

Fixing the mic would not fix echo, and that is the stronger argument for different hardware. The Brio's own descriptor declares its mic as "Speakerphone, **no echo reduction**" ([linux-hardware.org probe](https://linux-hardware.org/?probe=ff03f6aa77&log=lsusb)). In Android WebView, echo cancellation uses the device's built-in canceller only if Android reports one (`AcousticEchoCanceler.isAvailable()`). Otherwise it falls back to WebRTC's software canceller ([audio_manager_android.cc](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/refs/heads/main/media/audio/android/audio_manager_android.cc); [media_stream_audio_processor_options.cc](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/refs/heads/main/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/mediastream/media_stream_audio_processor_options.cc)), which can only cancel audio played through the browser's own WebRTC calls. A Chromium engineer notes that on devices without a hardware canceller, "non-WebRTC audio will leak", and users report that sound played through Web Audio is not cancelled ([Chromium issue 40504498](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/40504498)). WhatsApp's engine plays the other person's voice through its own JavaScript audio drivers ([hisalman.in](https://hisalman.in/blog/reverse-engineering-whatsapp-calls)), not through browser WebRTC, so the browser's canceller probably can't hear it. That is unverified, and nobody knows whether the WASM engine runs its own echo cancellation. Sony's audio post-processing adds delay and distortion that weaken any software canceller further. WhatsApp itself warns that "using a separate external microphone and speaker devices might cause an echo" ([WhatsApp FAQ](https://faq.whatsapp.com/2092488194268012)).

A USB speakerphone avoids all of that, because its own chip hears both what it plays and what its mics pick up, with a short, fixed delay. The **EMEET M0 Plus** (four mics, with an echo-cancelling "VoiceIA" algorithm) costs **₹5,799** on Amazon.in ([Amazon.in](https://www.amazon.in/Conference-Speakerphone-Reduction-Bluetooth-Compatible/dp/B0BVZLS5GZ)). The **Jabra Speak 510**, with "in-built echo cancellation" ([Jabra techsheet](https://www.jabra.com/_/media/Jabra_VXi_Product-Documentation/Jabra-SPEAK-410-Series/technical-specifications/RevB/Jabra-Speak-410-Techsheet-A4-290420-Web.pdf)), costs **₹14,081** on Amazon and ₹18,871 on Flipkart ([Smartprix](https://www.smartprix.com/mobile_speakers/jabra-speak-510-25w-portable-bluetooth-speaker-p1707foayhu1)).

The catch is audio routing. The speakerphone only cancels what it plays itself, so the call audio must come out of the speakerphone, not the TV speakers. That works in principle: one owner of a 2020 BRAVIA sends audio to a USB DAC ([Sony Community](https://community.sony.co.uk/t5/android-tv/usb-audio-support-usb-dac-for-sony-bravia-android-tv-kd-49xh8096/td-p/3881043)). Whether the VH21 offers USB audio output is unverified. Check `adb shell dumpsys media.audio_policy` during a call. If needed, the app can force the route with `AudioManager.setCommunicationDevice()`, which Chromium already calls during calls ([AudioManagerAndroid.java](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/refs/heads/main/media/base/android/java/src/org/chromium/media/AudioManagerAndroid.java)).

With a speakerphone that cancels echo itself, try `echoCancellation: false` in tvchat's audio settings. Running two cancellers at once can produce half-duplex, walkie-talkie-style audio, according to a vendor cheat sheet of medium reliability ([WebRTC echo cancellation cheat sheet](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/64e8910adc5a63966a68acea/6a21c5170c93af2bcdf13b91_6a21c5151e697d8889816c5b_webrtc-echo-cancellation-integration-cheat-sheet.pdf)). Turning off the browser's processing may also trim the ~0.2-core audio thread.

The budget alternative is a **Boya BY-LM40** USB clip-on mic at **₹1,859** ([Roy Imaging](https://royimaging.com/boya-by-lm40)). It leaves echo to software, so keep echo cancellation on, keep the TV volume moderate, and clip the mic to the speaker's collar. The remaining options don't work:
- Google says the remote's mic can't be used for audio on Meet calls ([Android TV Help](https://support.google.com/androidtv/answer/10079968?hl=en)).
- Sony documents Bluetooth audio output only, not headset microphones ([Sony India](https://www.sony.co.in/electronics/support/articles/00159290)).
- Sony lists many BRAVIA Google TV models where the built-in mic is not available ([Sony support](https://www.sony.com/electronics/support/articles/00231664)).

Using a phone's browser as a wireless mic is possible in theory: tvchat would override getUserMedia to take audio from the phone. Nobody has built it, and the phone's echo canceller would not hear the TV's output. The same speakerphone also serves the native-app route.

## Ranked plan: a day of tuning, an hour of native testing, then a hardware decision

The order below follows cost and what each step proves. The first step decides whether steps 3 and 4 can work at all, and the native test decides whether to keep investing in the TV.

Buying hardware becomes the better answer when any of three signals remains after steps 1–4:
- calls still keep the TV above about three busy cores;
- WebView's renderer still crashes from running out of memory;
- the far end still sees jerky video while capture holds a steady 15 fps.

Each of these means the TV's chip, not the wrapper, is the limit. A fourth trigger is the goal itself. If "good quality" means 720p or better, no app on this TV can deliver it, because the Sony's camera stack gives only about 11.5 fps at 720p. In any of these cases, the box to buy is an N100 mini PC or a spare laptop, not an Android TV box, with phone mirroring as the free stopgap. The Shield is the only Android TV box worth considering. It costs more than a mini PC, has no Indian channel and still relies on unofficial WhatsApp routes. Keep the linked-device limit in mind: the wrapper, the native TV app and a PC each take one of WhatsApp's four slots.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. The jitter is less a WebView bug than a mismatch: a desktop-tuned engine, with a 20-worker pool and a quick ramp-up to HD, running on a phone-class TV whose camera tops out around 16 fps. The wrapper's real job is to make WhatsApp behave like a client on a cheap phone: feed it small frames and draw few pixels. On this TV, success looks like steady 360p–480p video at about 15 fps. Meta reports typical call bitrates of 10–400 kbps, a range where sub-HD video is the norm, so this is probably not far from what phone-to-phone WhatsApp calls deliver anyway (an inference). HD is a hardware decision, and the cheapest hardware that delivers it is a PC that runs desktop Chrome and sees the Brio's full set of functions, not an Android TV box.

The routes also differ in how long they are likely to keep working, which the cost comparison hides. The wrapper depends on WhatsApp continuing to accept a fake desktop browser on an unsupported operating system. The native TV app depends on an undocumented camera code path. A WhatsApp update could break either without warning, while the phone and the mini PC sit inside WhatsApp's supported setup. That makes the speakerphone the one purchase you won't regret if you stay on the TV, since every TV route needs it. It also makes the mini PC worth keeping in mind as a fallback, even if tuning works today.
